# Naming the Structure: Why Career Advice Alone Falls Short

![Five professionals in business attire walking away down a bright modern office corridor, an African woman in a grey suit at the centre carrying a folder, passing between glass turnstile gates toward an open doorway](/blog/images/naming-the-structure-career-advice.jpg)

Some people are working against forces that have nothing to do with their talent or effort.

Career development has a tendency to be very optimistic and very individualistic.

But the systemic patterns in who gets hired, promoted, funded, mentored, believed, and included can get in the way.

They are features of systems.

Career development that doesn't account for them isn't just incomplete. It can be actively harmful, leaving people to conclude the problem is in them when it's in the structure.

Naming structure doesn't mean surrendering to it.

It means seeing it clearly enough to navigate it honestly.

## Why the individualistic version sells so well

Most career advice is built on a simple promise: if you improve yourself enough, the outcomes will follow. Get the certification. Refine the CV. Practise the interview. Build the network.

That advice is not worthless. Skills matter, preparation matters, and there is real value in doing the things that are within your control.

But notice what the framework quietly assumes. It assumes a level playing field, then locates every disappointing outcome in the person who experienced it. If you did not get the callback, something in your application needed work. If you were passed over, you needed more visibility. The model has no room for the possibility that the process itself was not neutral.

## The evidence that the process is not neutral

This is not a matter of opinion. It has been measured.

In a well known field experiment, researchers sent thousands of fictional resumes to real job advertisements in Boston and Chicago. The resumes were identical in quality. The only thing randomly varied was whether the applicant's name sounded white or African American [1].

Resumes with white sounding names received about 50 percent more callbacks. The researchers calculated that having a white sounding name was worth roughly as much as an additional eight years of experience on a CV. The gap held across occupation, industry and employer size. Crucially, higher quality resumes produced a bigger boost for white applicants than for African American applicants, so doing more work did not close the gap [1].

Read that last finding again, because it is the part that matters most. Improvement did not neutralise the disadvantage. It was rewarded, just less.

## It compounds rather than appearing once

A single biased screening is a setback. What makes it structural is that the same patterns repeat at every stage.

Research on promotion inside a real technology company tracked advancement across race, gender and job level over five years. Even after controlling for performance ratings and time in the previous position, odds of promotion varied in ways that reproduced existing racial and gender hierarchies [2]. The study describes a glass ceiling for some groups, a step stool for others, and a sticky floor for Black, Latinx, Native and Pacific Islander women on both the technical and management tracks [2].

Similar compounding shows up in academia, where hiring rank, promotion speed, attrition and age penalties stack on top of one another rather than acting separately [3].

The pattern is what matters. One unfair decision is bad luck. A pattern that repeats at hiring, then promotion, then funding, then sponsorship is a feature of the system, and no amount of individual effort at one stage corrects it at the others.

## The harm of refusing to name it

Here is where the optimistic version stops being merely incomplete and starts causing damage.

Research on meritocracy beliefs found that when people are primed with the idea that status reflects merit, they respond to rejection by blaming themselves rather than recognising discrimination. In the experiments, women primed with meritocracy were more likely to attribute an ambiguous rejection to their own poor work than to bias [4].

The same research showed the effect holds for both high and low status groups, and that meritocracy beliefs reduce the tendency of disadvantaged groups to perceive themselves as facing discrimination at all [4].

The psychological cost is documented in education research too. When outcomes are framed as purely deserved, failure gets reinterpreted as a stable personal deficiency rather than a response to structural conditions, producing self blame, stigma and genuine distress [5]. And the effect is not evenly distributed. Research on how meritocracy plays out in the Global South, where colonial histories and racial hierarchies persist, describes it reframing historically produced deprivation as individual failure [6].

That is the mechanism behind the harm. Not that hard work is pointless, but that a framework with no place for structure guarantees that people experiencing structural barriers will draw the wrong conclusion about themselves.

## Naming structure is not surrendering to it

There is a common worry that acknowledging systemic barriers makes people passive. The evidence points the other way.

In the promotion study, the point of measuring bias by race, gender and position was to make the pattern visible so it could be corrected [2]. Research on faculty advancement found that when underrepresented candidates were evaluated jointly with others rather than in isolated sequential decisions, racial disparities were mitigated [7]. In other words, naming the mechanism pointed directly at an intervention.

The same logic applies to you, individually. You cannot advocate for a fairer process, choose where to spend your effort, or decide when a lack of progress is information about the environment rather than information about you, if you have not first admitted that the environment can be the problem.

## What honest navigation looks like

**Separate the two questions.** When something does not go your way, ask both: what could I have done differently, and what part of this was not about me? Answering only the first is the mistake the individualistic model trains you to make.

**Look for patterns, not incidents.** One rejection is data of limited value. Rejections that cluster by stage, by team, or by who tends to advance and who tends to leave are structural signals worth reading.

**Ask what the process actually measures.** If the criteria are vague, discretionary and applied by a small group without structure, outcomes will track relationships and familiarity more than merit. Knowing that tells you which levers to pull, and which are not worth pulling.

**Find your read.** The most useful career insight in a biased system often comes from someone a few steps ahead who shares your context. They can tell you what the environment is actually like, which is different from what it claims to be.

**Keep your evidence.** Documents, performance records, written feedback, results. Structure is easier to name, and to challenge, when you have specifics rather than a feeling.

**Build with others, not only alone.** Individual strategy has limits by definition. Collective knowledge, shared information and sponsorship across a group are how patterns get named and eventually changed.

## Seeing it clearly

None of this is an argument for despair, and it is not an argument against effort. It is an argument against a story that has been quietly harmful for a long time: that outcomes are a pure measure of the person who received them.

Some of what stands between you and where you want to be is you. Some of it is the system. Being able to tell the difference is not pessimism. It is accuracy, and it is the beginning of any honest plan.
