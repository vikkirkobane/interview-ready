# Whose Definition of Success Are You Actually Working Toward?

![African professional holding a handwritten list at a bright office window, reflecting on their own definition of success](/blog/images/whose-definition-of-success.jpg)

One question I keep coming back to: whose definition of success are you operating under?

It sounds simple, but it is not.

Most of us have not chosen our definition of success consciously. It was handed to us. By industry, by family, by the cultural moment we came of age in, by the mentors who shaped our sense of what was worth pursuing.

Try it right now. Think about your industry or organization. What is success, by that world's definition? And when you hold that definition up to the light, does it still look like yours?

## The definition you inherited

Nobody sits you down and hands you a definition of success. It accumulates. You absorb it from the promotion that gets celebrated in your team, from the relative who asks whether the job is "serious", from the classmates whose careers get described as impressive, from the version of a life that your industry quietly treats as the finished article.

By the time you are making real decisions, that definition is already running in the background. It shapes which opportunities you say yes to, which you dismiss without much thought, and what you feel when someone your age announces a title you do not have.

Research on career success makes the split explicit. Scholars distinguish extrinsic success, which is observable and comparable, things like pay, status and advancement, from intrinsic success, which is your own subjective evaluation of your working life [1]. Both matter. The problem is that most of us are far more fluent in the extrinsic version, because it is the one that gets measured, announced and applauded by other people.

## Success is not a universal standard

One of the more useful findings in this research is how much definitions of success vary across cultures. A study of 269 professional services employees across 15 countries found that the way people define career success tracks the cultural values they grew up within, even after controlling for gender, occupation, job level and national wealth [2].

In cultures that reward individual achievement and performance, success is more often described in terms of advancement, pay and recognition. In more socially supportive cultures, people more often describe success in terms of relationships, contribution, satisfaction and security.

Neither list is wrong. But the point is sharp: what feels like an obvious, objective standard of success is usually a local one, dressed up as universal. If you are measuring yourself against a definition built for a different context, or for a different person's life, you can succeed by that measure and still feel like you are losing.

## The comparison trap

There is a second force at work, and it is one of the most studied in organizational psychology. Social comparison is the habit of evaluating your own accomplishments, salary, progress or potential against other people's [3]. It is not a character flaw. It is wired in, and workplaces make it almost impossible to avoid, since you are surrounded by peers whose progress is visible.

What makes it costly is the direction it usually takes. Research consistently finds that upward comparisons, looking at people who appear to be doing better, are more common than downward ones. Upward comparison can inspire you. It can also quietly redefine your goals without your consent, until you are chasing something you never chose simply because someone ahead of you is chasing it too.

A study of part time MBA students found that 68 percent of participants used criteria based on other people, such as satisfaction with their income relative to their peers, when judging their own career success, and that these other referent measures explained a meaningful share of how successful they felt overall [4]. In other words, a substantial part of how successful you feel may have little to do with your actual life and a lot to do with the gap between you and the people you happen to stand next to.

## Why the inherited definition holds so hard

Psychologists who study motivation describe the difference between pursuing something because it genuinely matters to you and pursuing it to protect your sense of worth, or to avoid guilt, anxiety or shame [5]. The second kind still produces effort, sometimes extraordinary effort, but it drains rather than fuels.

That helps explain something familiar. People who reach the inherited version of success, the title, the salary, the recognition, often describe a strange flatness rather than triumph. The goal was never theirs to feel satisfied by. It was borrowed, so its achievement settles someone else's account.

Add context pressure and the cost compounds. Research on how young people define success shows that family expectations, gender norms and socioeconomic circumstances all shape what a person believes success even is, and that for those from less advantaged backgrounds the definitions on offer can sit in real tension with their own circumstances [6]. When the available definition of success was not designed with your life in mind, measuring yourself against it will feel like failing even when you are doing well.

## How to examine your own definition

This is not an argument for ignoring money or ambition. It is an argument for knowing which parts you actually chose.

**Write down your definition before you judge it.** Not the aspirational version, the working one. What would have to be true in three years for you to feel that your career is going well? Be specific.

**Then ask where each line came from.** For every item, note who taught you it mattered. Industry, family, a mentor, a cohort you came up in. Some items will survive that question easily. Others will suddenly look borrowed.

**Name what the appraisal is measuring.** In your organization, what gets noticed and rewarded? Is that the same thing as the work you are proud of? If the gap is large, you are being measured by someone else's definition all day, and it is worth deciding how much weight to give it.

**Check your comparison set.** Whose progress do you track most closely? If your sense of doing well depends entirely on staying ahead of a specific group of people, that is not a standard, it is a race with no finish line.

**Revisit it deliberately.** Your definition will shift with experience, and it should. The goal is that the shift is something you do, rather than something that happens to you by default.

## The question worth keeping

Whose definition of success are you operating under?

Not because the answer is always wrong. Often parts of it will be exactly right, chosen consciously and worth pursuing hard. But you cannot know which parts those are until you hold the whole thing up to the light and look.

And when you do, that definition stops being a silent inheritance and becomes a decision. Which is the only form of it that ever really belonged to you.
