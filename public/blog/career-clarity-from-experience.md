# Career Clarity Comes from Experience, Not from Thinking Harder

You have probably done this already. You sat down with a notebook, listed your strengths, ranked your values, and tried to reason your way to a decision about what to do next. And after an hour, or a week, or a year, you still did not have an answer.

That is not a failure of intelligence or effort. It is a failure of method. Clarity about work rarely arrives through more thinking. It arrives through contact with reality.

## Why thinking alone cannot solve this

There is a useful line from the Stanford life design work of Bill Burnett and Dave Evans: you cannot solve your future because you do not have the data yet [1]. No amount of reflection will generate information you have never encountered. If you have not done the work, met the people, or stood inside the environment, your imagination is filling the gaps with guesses, and most of those guesses are borrowed from other people's expectations.

Psychologists studying career decision making have recognised this for decades. Career decision theories consistently find that self knowledge grows through learning experiences, not through introspection alone. Social Cognitive Career Theory, one of the most widely supported frameworks in vocational psychology, holds that your beliefs about what you are capable of are built from what you have actually tried and observed, not from what you have daydreamed about [2].

## What experience gives you that thought cannot

When you take the class, volunteer on the project, or spend an afternoon shadowing someone in the field, you generate something a brainstorm can never produce: a genuine reaction.

You find out that you hate the parts you expected to love. You discover that the thing you were intimidated by is actually energising. You notice which conversations you keep replaying in your head on the way home. That data is yours, it is specific, and it is far more reliable than a list of values written in the abstract.

One of the more striking findings in career research is that around 80 percent of people report that unexpected chance events, a meeting, an accidental introduction, an invitation they nearly declined, shaped the direction of their careers more than any formal plan did [3]. John Krumboltz, who developed what is now called Planned Happenstance Theory, did not conclude from this that planning is pointless. He concluded the opposite: since unplanned events matter so much, you should deliberately act in ways that increase the odds of running into them.

That is the core insight. Exploration is not the absence of a plan. It is the most effective plan available when you genuinely do not know yet.

## The prototype mindset

Burnett and Evans describe the technique as prototyping: building a small, low stakes version of a possible future so you can test it before committing [1]. Among the examples they give:

- A conversation with someone who already does the work you are curious about
- Shadowing a professional for a day
- A short exploratory project, even unpaid
- A scaled down version of the career you imagine, rather than the full leap

Notice how modest each of these is. None of them require you to quit anything or announce a new identity. They require a few hours and a willingness to be a beginner in public for an afternoon.

Krumboltz identified five attitudes that make this work: curiosity, persistence, flexibility, optimism, and a willingness to take risks [4]. Of those, curiosity is the one people most often suppress. We learn to filter out anything that does not appear directly useful, and in doing so we close the doors that chance would otherwise open.

## What this looks like in practice

If you are early in your career, or considering a change, the following moves are small enough to start this month:

**Talk to three people who do work you find interesting.** Not to ask for a job. To ask what their day actually looks like, what surprised them, and what they would tell someone considering it. Keep it to fifteen or twenty minutes and be respectful of their time [5].

**Take one thing you would normally dismiss as not relevant to you.** A workshop, a short course, a community project. The value is not the certificate, it is the reaction it produces in you.

**Build something small and real.** A portfolio piece, a volunteer deliverable, a side project with an actual user. Finished work in the world teaches you more about fit than a year of planning.

**Write down what you notice, not what you conclude.** Capture the moments that felt alive and the ones that felt draining. Patterns will emerge across experiences that no single one reveals.

## You do not have to know before you start

The pressure to already know what you want is one of the most common reasons people stay stuck. It frames the decision as a single exam you must pass with no information, which is precisely why it feels paralysing.

You do not have to know. You have to experience enough to be able to name it. Clarity is not the starting line of a career, it is a byproduct of movement.

Every experience you accumulate becomes a point of reference. Enough of them, and the shape of what fits you stops being a mystery and becomes obvious.
